Checkpoint replay and bounded recovery¶

Checkpointing and resuming pipelines says that a checkpoint records which work chunks were finished and which were still in progress, and that a restore rebuilds operator state by re-processing a bounded number of recent chunks. This page is about how Zephon works out which chunks those are, which is the trickiest part of the whole system to get right.

Why there is no sample index. A map-style loader checkpoints by writing down an index, and Zephon cannot, because a Pipeline that splits documents, packs fragments and reorders records has no index to write down. Progress is spread across in-flight operator state, partially filled bins, and records that have been reordered relative to their inputs. We could replay the whole Pipeline from the start on every resume, which would be correct, but crashing at step 100,000 would then mean redoing 100,000 steps of data processing before you get one new batch.

So Zephon sits in between. What we aim for is to replay only the chunks that were still in flight at checkpoint time, which is a handful of them regardless of how far into training you are.

What a checkpoint holds. Three things, per lane:

  • Inflight chunks, serialized in full: their sample pointers, component order, and seed. On restore these are deserialized directly and the WorkSource is never asked to regenerate them. They are the only chunks that get replayed through the operator pipeline.

  • Epoch boundaries, for pipelines that need them: the list of sentinel chunk IDs marking flush points. This can include a trailing boundary just past the last inflight chunk, which preserves the flush point between replayed data and newly fetched data. On restore the sentinels are re-injected at these positions so accumulators flush where they flushed originally.

  • WorkSource state, so next_chunk() can resume producing new chunks from the right place. The WorkSource is not replayed and not re-advanced, because load_state_dict() restores its position directly.

What goes into that last one is up to the implementation. For StaticMixtureWorkSource the sample sequence is fully determined by the datasets, the seed, the shuffle knobs and the lane, so the state is a compact set of per-dataset cursor positions plus mixture bookkeeping, and restoring it is instantaneous no matter how far into training you are. WorkSources and work chunks covers what those cursors are and what the rest of the contract requires of a source you write yourself.

Alongside these sit the replay cursor per lane, the lane progress pointers, and the round-robin position discussed in Elastic determinism.

One thing is deliberately absent. The per-offset completion bitmaps are runtime-only, and Zephon rebuilds them as the replayed chunks flow through the Pipeline and re-trigger contributor tracking. That keeps your checkpoints small, at the cost of making restore time proportional to the number of inflight chunks.

Eviction. What keeps the inflight set small is eviction. Once every sample derived from a chunk has been delivered, the chunk leaves the set, and a checkpoint taken afterwards neither stores nor replays it.

Lane 0 inflight chunks over time:

  step 100:  [ chunk 3 | chunk 4 | chunk 5 ]
  step 200:  [ chunk 4 | chunk 5 | chunk 6 ]    chunk 3 evicted
  step 300:  [ chunk 5 | chunk 6 | chunk 7 ]    chunk 4 evicted

Without eviction the inflight set grows without bound and every resume replays everything, so the only real difficulty is timing: evict too early and replay loses data it needed, evict too late and your checkpoints bloat.

Zephon picks a strategy at compile time by looking at every operator’s preserves_cursor_order trait:

Pipeline compiled
       │
  Do all operators preserve cursor order?
      ╱ ╲
    yes   no  (packing, shuffling, mixture correction)
     │     │
  Monotone  Epoch-based
  eviction  eviction

Monotone eviction. When every operator preserves cursor order, records come out in the same chunk order they went in. If the most recently delivered record belongs to chunk N, then everything from chunks below N has already gone out and those chunks evict immediately, so a simple watermark is all we need. Most pipelines without shuffling or packing take this path.

Epoch-based eviction. Packing, shuffling and mixture correction break that watermark in two ways, and the second is much worse than the first.

The obvious problem is reordering. A packed record can combine fragments from chunks 5 and 7, and chunk 5’s last sample can be delivered after something from chunk 7, so a rule of “evict everything below the most recent chunk” would drop chunk 5 while part of it is still in flight. Per-offset tracking fixes that one.

The deeper problem is accumulator history dependence. The packer’s bin state, meaning which bins exist and how full they are, depends on the entire history of what it has seen rather than just on what is currently in the bins. After a chunk’s samples have all left the bins, their influence on where subsequent samples land persists. Two packers starting from different states and fed identical input may never converge. With two bins of capacity 10 and an input stream of [3, 3, 3, …], starting from (10, 10) versus (10, 5) gives the same periodic emission cycle, permanently phase-shifted.

That leaves us with a direct conflict. Eviction wants to discard consumed chunks to free memory, while deterministic replay wants to reconstruct the accumulator state the original run had, and that state was shaped by inputs from exactly the chunks eviction wants to discard. Replaying from a checkpoint would start the packer empty instead of mid-stream and produce different packed records, and the replay filter’s target cursor might never appear at all. The same argument applies to mixture correction, whose deficit state depends on emission history, and to shuffling, whose buffers span chunk boundaries.

Our resolution is the flush sentinel. Every flush_every_k_chunks chunks per lane, Zephon injects one that forces the history-dependent accumulators to emit everything and reset to a clean state, as described in Operators and runners. Sentinels divide the stream into epochs, and each epoch starts from a state that replay can reproduce without any of the preceding chunks:

Epoch 0          Epoch 1          Epoch 2
[chunk 0, 1]  →  [chunk 2, 3]  →  [chunk 4, 5]  → ...
             ↑                ↑
          sentinel         sentinel
           (flush)          (flush)

How pipelines run covers the trade that flush_every_k_chunks controls. What that page leaves implicit is what a flush costs at the operator level, since each one interrupts an accumulator mid-stride: the packer emits partially filled bins and pays for them in padding, the shuffle buffer emits a smaller window and randomizes less, and the mixture corrector drains mid-rebalance.

On a non-monotonic Pipeline, setting flush_every_k_chunks=0 is an error, because without sentinels the accumulator state depends on unbounded history and we cannot evict anything safely. On a monotone one, setting a positive value gets you a warning and Zephon forces the value back to 0, since sentinels buy you nothing there and would perturb your batch boundaries for free.

Per-offset tracking. Within an epoch, Zephon tracks completion per offset. Each sample occupies a position in its chunk, and operators may split it into several children or merge children from different offsets into one packed record, so an offset only closes once all of its children have been delivered. Zephon learns that from the is_last_child flags on contributor metadata, and from tombstones when a filter drops the last child standing (see Records and sample lifecycles). A bitmap per chunk records which offsets are closed, with a popcount alongside so that “is this chunk complete?” stays O(1).

Chunk 5 (4 offsets):  [x] [x] [ ] [x]    3 of 4 closed
Chunk 6 (4 offsets):  [x] [x] [x] [x]    all closed, but not evicted
Chunk 7 (4 offsets):  [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ]    none closed

→ Nothing is evicted: 5 and 6 are in the same epoch, and 5 is incomplete.

Atomic per-epoch eviction. Chunks within an epoch share accumulator history, since where chunk 1’s samples landed in the bins depends on what chunk 0 put there, so evicting chunk 0 while chunk 1 is still inflight would break replay for exactly the reason above. An epoch therefore evicts atomically: all of its chunks, or none of them.

Zephon walks a lane’s recorded boundaries in ascending order and evicts contiguously from the bottom, stopping at the first epoch that is incomplete. The epoch boundaries are what make this safe across epochs, because evicting epoch 0 while epoch 1 is still running is fine precisely when epoch 1’s accumulators started empty after the sentinel flush.

Epoch 0 (chunks 0–1):  all offsets closed   → evict together
Epoch 1 (chunks 2–3):  still in progress    → keep
Epoch 2 (chunks 4–5):  not started          → keep

Only a lane’s own recorded boundaries authorize eviction for that lane. The restriction matters because sentinels are per-lane, so a boundary established when a different lane flushed says nothing about this lane’s accumulator history. Until a lane has recorded a boundary of its own, Zephon retains its entire open epoch even when every offset in it is closed, which means your early chunks stay inflight until the first sentinel arrives. We consider that a fair trade of a little memory for an eviction rule with no special cases, and the full Pipeline drain at end of stream handles whatever is left.

Cursor pinning adds the last constraint. The replay cursor is the identity of the last delivered record, and it has to point into a chunk that is still inflight or the replay filter cannot find its target on resume. If the cursor sits in an epoch that is otherwise eligible, Zephon stops there and leaves it alone, which resolves on its own within a few deliveries, as soon as a post-sentinel record goes out.

Replay on resume. When you restore and then iterate, Zephon runs the saved state back through the full Pipeline:

Checkpoint
  │  inflight chunks + replay cursor + WorkSource state + epoch boundaries, per lane
  v
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Engine source stream                                  │
│   Phase 1: yield restored inflight chunks, by chunk ID│
│   Phase 2: fetch new chunks from the restored source  │
└────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                         v  sample pointers
┌─── Operator pipeline (deterministic replay) ─────────┐
│  fetch → tokenize → pack → ensure_mixture → ...       │
└────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                         v  records
┌─── Replay filter ────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Per lane: drop until cursor == target, then emit     │
└────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                         v  new records only
                   Training loop

First the inflight chunks are deserialized back into the inflight set and each lane’s WorkSource state is restored. Zephon then yields those chunks before fetching anything new, re-injecting flush sentinels at the stored boundary positions so that the accumulators flush exactly where they did before. Those chunks flow through the whole Pipeline, through fetch, tokenize, pack and batch, just as they originally did, and because the Pipeline is deterministic they produce the same records and rebuild the same accumulator state as a side effect. Finally the replay filter drops the prefix your training loop has already consumed.

The replay filter. Zephon inserts a replay filter into every Pipeline for you, so this is never something you add yourself. If the Pipeline has a batch operator the filter goes immediately before it, otherwise it is appended at the tail, and it takes its name from the operator it sits next to. You can see it in Pipeline.explain():

Stage[0] place=local runner=threads cap=8 mode=microbatches
  fetch@p4 -[in_q=16]-> tokenize@p4
  --[stage_out=16]-->
Stage[1] place=local runner=inline cap=1 mode=stream_items
  ensure_mixture@p1 -> batch_replay_filter@p1 -> batch@p1
  ==[final_prefetch=3]==> pipeline_end

On a fresh run it is a no-op that passes everything through. It only does anything on resume.

Putting it before batch is what makes batch(drop_last=True) the one operator allowed to stall at an epoch boundary. Because batching only emits complete batches, the consumer-visible checkpoint cut falls between whole batches, so on resume the prefix is dropped before batching and batching rebuilds the suffix from an empty buffer at the same boundary. No extra batch state has to be checkpointed, and that holds even when the pump thread has run far ahead of the consumer, because the cut is defined by delivered batches rather than by how much work the pump had done.

When the filter drops a record during the prefix it emits tombstones for it, which is not optional: without them, per-offset tracking would never close the offsets belonging to replayed records and their chunks could never be evicted.

The match is on equality, not on a threshold. At checkpoint time Zephon records, per lane, the SampleCursor of the most recently delivered record, and on resume the filter drops records until it sees a cursor equal to that target, drops the target too, and then forwards everything after it.

We use equality rather than <= because the tail of a Pipeline with shuffling does not arrive in cursor order:

Original run:   C2  C1  C3  C4  C5  ...
                        ↑ checkpoint here, saved target = C3

A threshold rule would drop C1, C2 and C3, which is correct, but it would also drop any later record whose cursor happens to fall below C3, and you never consumed those. The equality rule waits for C3 to reappear in the deterministic replay and forwards everything after it, which is correct whatever the output ordering looks like, because a cursor is a unique identity rather than an ordinal position.

What a custom operator owes replay. If your operator buffers across chunk boundaries it has to cooperate with all of the above, or Zephon will evict a chunk whose data your operator is still holding. The obligations are spelled out on the two pages before this one; gathered together, they are:

  • Set preserves_cursor_order=False in OpTraits if your operator reorders anything.

  • Propagate contributor metadata, and set is_last_child correctly.

  • Emit tombstones for dropped final fragments.

  • Implement flush(reset=True, lane_id=...) so that it fully resets that lane’s state. After a mid-stream flush your accumulator has to report no pending data for that lane, and the runner treats anything else as a contract violation and raises.

  • Do not plan on stalling. We support it for batch(drop_last=True) and nothing else.

Multi-rank aggregation. Each rank owns a subset of lanes, but a complete checkpoint has to cover all of them, so the ranks merge their states through shared coordination storage using a leader-follower protocol. That storage is either a shared directory (aggregate_dir, a local or shared filesystem or object storage) or a key-value store (aggregate_store, where "torch://" uses the TCPStore that torchrun already runs). The protocol is the same over both. The leader is global rank 0, DataLoader worker 0.

Leader                     shared storage                  Followers
  │                            │                               │
  │── announce round id ──────>│                               │
  │                            │<────── read round id ─────────│
  │── write own state ────────>│                               │
  │                            │<────── write states ──────────│
  │── wait: every rank covers  │                               │
  │   all its lanes? ─────────>│                               │
  │── close round              │                               │
  │   (delete announcement) ──>│                               │
  │── merge, publish merged ──>│                               │
  │── delete own state ───────>│                               │
  │                            │<────── wait for merged ───────│
  │                            │─────── read merged ──────────>│
  │                            │<── delete own state, ack ─────│
  │── wait for every ack ─────>│                               │
  │── delete acks, previous    │                               │
  │   round's merged ─────────>│                               │

The leader’s wait condition is stricter than it might look. It is not enough for the lanes reported across all ranks to add up to every one of the canonical_replicas: each rank has to report every lane its DP group owns, so that a model-parallel peer in the same DP group can’t stand in for a DataLoader worker that is running late. The leader also checks that every state it receives agrees with its own lane mapping, and raises on a mismatch rather than waiting for it to resolve itself.

aggregate_timeout_s is one budget for the whole round, not a limit per wait. If it runs out while the leader is still collecting states, the error tells you which ranks and lanes are missing, which is usually enough to find the rank that forgot to call checkpoint(). If the leader fails before publishing the merged result, it publishes a failure notice instead, so the followers fail straight away rather than waiting out the timeout.

Because the leader waits for every ack, nobody is still reading the merged result when the leader’s checkpoint() call returns. We keep it around anyway as a marker that the round is closed, and delete it once the next round has been fully acknowledged or the Pipeline shuts down; if the acks time out, it stays for the followers that haven’t read it yet. States are written with msgpack and zstd by default. Against object storage the polling backs off exponentially, capped at five seconds, while the TCPStore uses its own blocking waits.
